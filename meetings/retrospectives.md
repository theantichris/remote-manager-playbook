# Retrospectives

Retrospectives give us an opportunity to reflect on how we're working, celebrate what's going well, identify what's getting in our way, and figure out how we can improve.

The goal isn't to assign blame or create a list of complaints. It's to learn from our experiences and make meaningful changes that help us work better together.

A retrospective is only valuable if we do something with what we've learned.

## Principles

- **Create a safe environment.** Everyone should feel comfortable sharing their experiences, concerns, and ideas without fear of blame or judgment.
- **Focus on systems, not individuals.** Look at processes, communication, decisions, and the circumstances that contribute to problems. Assume positive intent while remaining accountable for our actions.
- **Celebrate successes.** Understanding what's working is just as important as identifying what isn't. We should recognize the behaviors and practices we want to continue.
- **Encourage participation.** Everyone experiences the work differently. Make room for quieter voices and different perspectives.
- **Focus on improvement.** We don't need to solve every problem in one meeting. A small number of meaningful changes is better than a long list of things we'll never address.

## When to Hold a Retrospective

Retrospectives can happen regularly or following a significant event, such as completing a project, reaching a milestone, or navigating a challenging period.

I don't believe there's one correct cadence. Teams should find a rhythm that provides opportunities to reflect without creating unnecessary meetings.

We also shouldn't wait for a retrospective to address something that needs immediate attention.

## Preparing

A little preparation helps make our conversations more productive.

Before the retrospective:

1. Review action items and improvements from previous retrospectives.
2. Share any relevant context, such as project outcomes, team feedback, or delivery challenges.
3. Give everyone an opportunity to reflect and add topics beforehand.
4. Identify someone to facilitate the conversation.

Allowing people to contribute asynchronously can help everyone organize their thoughts and give people who process information differently an opportunity to participate.

Use the [Retro Notes template](../templates/retro-notes.md) to capture reflections, decisions, and improvements.

## Running the Retrospective

### 1. Review Previous Improvements

Start by reviewing the changes we agreed to make during previous retrospectives.

- Did we follow through?
- Did the changes have the intended effect?
- What did we learn?
- Should we continue, adjust, or stop doing something?

This helps us understand whether our efforts are actually improving how we work.

### 2. Gather Feedback

Give everyone an opportunity to share their observations.

Encourage people to discuss what's going well, what's challenging, and what they think we could improve.

Silent writing can be useful here, allowing everyone time to reflect before we begin discussing.

### 3. Identify Themes

Group related feedback and look for patterns.

Not every observation requires an action, and not every problem needs to be solved immediately.

Focus on the things that have the greatest impact on our ability to work effectively.

Voting can help prioritize topics, but it shouldn't replace a conversation about what's most important.

### 4. Understand the Problems

Before jumping into solutions, make sure we understand what's happening and why.

Ask questions, explore contributing factors, and consider different perspectives.

Try to identify the underlying issues rather than simply addressing symptoms.

Avoid turning the discussion into an exercise in assigning blame.

### 5. Agree on Improvements

Choose one or two meaningful changes we can make.

For each improvement, identify:

- **Action:** What are we going to change or try?
- **Owner:** Who is responsible for moving it forward?
- **Outcome:** What improvement do we expect?
- **Follow-up:** When will we evaluate whether it worked?

Treat improvements as experiments. We don't have to know whether something will work before trying it, but we should be willing to evaluate the results and adjust.

## Choosing a Format

Different formats work better for different teams and situations. We don't need to use the same approach every time.

A few options include:

- **Start, Stop, Continue:** What should we begin doing, stop doing, or keep doing?
- **Went Well, Could Improve:** What worked, and what could we do differently?
- **Timeline:** Walk through significant events to understand how a project or situation unfolded.
- **Experiment Review:** Evaluate changes we've already made and decide what to do next.

The format should support the conversation, not dictate it.

## Following Through

The most important part of a retrospective happens after the meeting.

Document the improvements we've agreed to make and track them wherever the team manages its work.

Make sure action items have clear owners and aren't forgotten once everyone returns to their regular responsibilities.

At the next retrospective, revisit those improvements and discuss what happened.

If we consistently identify the same problems without making progress, we need to understand what's preventing us from addressing them.

## What Success Looks Like

A healthy retrospective gives everyone an opportunity to be heard, helps us understand how we're working, and leads to meaningful improvements.

We should leave with a better understanding of our successes, challenges, and what we're going to do differently.

**The goal isn't to have a great retrospective. It's to become a better team because we had one.**
