# Pants Based Sizing

Think about the size of your work like a good pair of pants. Both need to feel comfortable.

If you put too little food on your plate your pants will be too loose. You end up with a lot of extra room and they'll fall down, embarrassing you.

If you put too much food on your plate your pants will get too tight. They will feel constricting and you won't have room to be flexible.

When planning your work, divide your food into meals and cake. **Meals represent the required work, while cake represents the nice-to-haves.** You need to finish your meal before getting to the cake, but if you take on too much required work, you'll never have room for the things you'd like to do.

## Practical Guidance

- **Preferred size.** Aim for tickets that can be completed in 2–3 days of focused work. This is a guideline, not a hard rule. Larger work should be broken into smaller, manageable pieces when possible. If we don't understand the work well enough to size it, we should investigate before making commitments.
- **Work in progress (WIP).** Limit how much work you take on at once. Focus on finishing what you've started before taking on something new. Starting more work doesn't mean we're accomplishing more. Protecting our capacity helps us maintain focus, respond to unexpected needs, and deliver meaningful results.
- **Split by outcome.** When work is too large to fit comfortably, break it into smaller pieces that deliver meaningful value. Whenever possible, each piece should produce something we can test, demonstrate, or learn from independently. Focus on delivering incremental outcomes rather than simply dividing work into technical tasks.
- **Acceptance criteria.** Clearly describe what success looks like so everyone understands when the work is complete. Focus on the expected behavior or outcome rather than prescribing how the solution should be implemented. Good acceptance criteria should be specific enough to verify while leaving room for engineers to determine the best approach.
- **Definition of done.** Before starting work, agree on what needs to happen for it to be considered complete. For code, that might include passing tests, merged changes, updated documentation, and a successful release. For research or design work, it might mean documented findings or a decision. The important thing is that everyone understands what done means and that we've achieved the intended outcome.
- **Anti-patterns.** Avoid large, poorly defined pieces of work, unclear ownership, and dependencies that leave people blocked. Don't commit to so much required work that there's no room for unexpected challenges or nice-to-haves. Good sizing should help us maintain focus, deliver value, and leave enough flexibility to adapt when things change.
