# Project Ownership and Weekly Updates

The goal is to have clear ownership, useful visibility, and fewer surprises without creating more meetings or layers of approval.

## Give Each Project a DRI

Every project should have one directly responsible individual (DRI). This should usually be someone close enough to the work to explain the decisions, trade-offs, risks, and progress.

The DRI owns the outcome, not every task. They coordinate with other contributors and stakeholders, make decisions within agreed boundaries, and ask for help when they need it. Calling someone a DRI does not mean leaving them to figure everything out alone.

When establishing ownership, make sure everyone knows:

1. Who the DRI is and where that information lives.
1. The intended outcome, current scope, next milestone, and target date.
1. Which decisions the DRI can make and which need partners or leadership involved.
1. Who can help unblock the work and how to reach them.

## Use a Weekly Written Update

Each DRI should publish a short update in the project's existing source of truth. Consistency matters more than a particular day or tool.

Include:

- **Status:** On track, at risk, or off track, with a brief explanation.
- **Target date and scope:** What they are and what changed since last week.
- **Progress:** What moved forward or what we learned.
- **Next steps:** The next meaningful work or decision.
- **Risks, blockers, and asks:** What's in the way, who can help, and when.
- **Evidence:** A demo, PR, design, document, or other artifact people can inspect.

Use the [Project Weekly Update](../templates/project-weekly-update.md) template as a starting point. An update should help someone understand the work without scheduling a status meeting. AI can help draft an update, but the DRI is still responsible for checking the facts and publishing it.

## What Leaders Owe the DRI

Leadership is responsible for establishing direction and decision boundaries, then following through with support.

- Read the updates, including the ones from healthy projects.
- Look for changes in dates, scope, risk, and dependencies.
- Ask questions when the status and evidence don't match.
- Remove blockers outside the DRI's control.
- Coach when communication or decision-making needs work.
- Resist the urge to take every decision back.

The point of reading an update is to understand what's happening and help, not to create another approval process.

## When a Conversation Is Better

Written updates are the default for visibility, not a replacement for collaboration. Use a conversation or bookable office hours when a decision needs discussion, a risk is hard to explain, or someone needs coaching or feedback.

Either the DRI or a leader can initiate the discussion. An invitation is not automatically an escalation. Urgent blockers should be raised immediately instead of waiting for the next update.

## What Good Looks Like

- People know who owns the project and what success means.
- Date, scope, and risk changes are visible early.
- The work itself is easy to inspect.
- DRIs can work directly with stakeholders and leaders.
- Decisions happen at the right level without unnecessary routing.
- Problems lead to support and learning instead of blame.

This approach is informed by James Stanier's [Put a name on it](https://theengineeringmanager.substack.com/p/put-a-name-on-it).
