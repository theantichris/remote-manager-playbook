# DRI Weekly Rhythm

Clear ownership helps teams move faster, make better decisions, and avoid unnecessary coordination.

I believe people should have the context, authority, and support to own meaningful outcomes without needing a manager involved in every decision.

A lightweight weekly rhythm makes that possible by giving everyone visibility into progress, risks, and decisions.

**The goal is fewer surprises, clearer ownership, and faster decisions, not more reporting.**

This approach is based on James Stanier's [*Put a name on it*](https://theengineeringmanager.substack.com/p/put-a-name-on-it).

## Principles

- **One name per project.** Every meaningful initiative should have one clearly identified directly responsible individual (DRI).
- **Ownership means accountability for the outcome.** The DRI doesn't have to do all the work. They coordinate contributions and keep the work moving.
- **Authority should accompany responsibility.** People need the ability to make decisions within clear boundaries.
- **Visibility enables autonomy.** Leaders should understand what's happening without requiring constant check-ins.
- **Support shouldn't require escalation through management.** DRIs should be able to collaborate directly with stakeholders and leaders.
- **Problems should be surfaced early.** Raising risks or asking for help is a sign of responsible ownership, not failure.

## Establishing Ownership

For each project or significant initiative:

1. Identify one DRI who is close enough to the work to understand and explain it.
2. Make that person visible in the project's existing source of truth.
3. Clarify the desired outcome, scope, target date, and next milestone.
4. Establish decision boundaries, including when collaboration or leadership involvement is necessary.
5. Ensure the DRI has direct access to the people and resources needed to succeed.

The DRI is the accountable point of contact, not the only person responsible for contributing.

We should also recognize that ownership is a skill people develop. Less-experienced DRIs may need coaching, feedback, and support as they grow into the responsibility.

## The Weekly Rhythm

The process has three parts.

### 1. DRIs Publish a Weekly Update

**Owner:** Each DRI

**Cadence:** Weekly, at a predictable time

**Location:** The project's existing tracking system

A good update should be brief, accurate, and understandable without requiring a separate meeting.

It should answer:

- **Status:** Is the project on track, at risk, or off track?
- **Date and scope:** Have our commitments changed?
- **Progress:** What moved forward or what did we learn?
- **Next steps:** What's going to happen next?
- **Risks and blockers:** What could prevent success, and what help is needed?
- **Evidence:** Where can someone see the actual work?

Changes to target dates or scope should be explicit, including the reason.

Link to demonstrations, pull requests, designs, or other artifacts when possible.

A status label alone isn't a useful update.

AI can help assemble information, but the DRI remains responsible for verifying and publishing it.

### 2. Leadership Reads the Updates

**Owner:** Leaders responsible for the relevant projects

**Cadence:** After the weekly updates are published

Leaders should read every update, not only those already marked as at risk.

Look for:

- Changes to delivery dates or scope.
- Progress that doesn't appear consistent with the reported status.
- Risks, blockers, or dependencies requiring support.
- Missing context or unclear next steps.
- Patterns that suggest a project needs additional attention.

Respond based on what the situation needs.

Sometimes an acknowledgment is enough. Other times, ask a question, provide feedback, remove a blocker, or arrange a focused conversation.

**The purpose is to understand and support the work, not take decisions away from the DRI.**

### 3. Use Two-Way Office Hours

Establish a regular, optional opportunity for DRIs and leaders to discuss important questions.

Office hours should work in both directions.

**DRI-initiated conversations:**

- Requesting feedback on an approach.
- Working through a trade-off.
- Seeking help with a dependency.
- Discussing a risk or difficult decision.
- Demonstrating progress and gathering input.

**Leader-initiated conversations:**

- Clarifying something in a project update.
- Offering support with a potential blocker.
- Discussing concerns about direction or scope.
- Providing coaching or additional context.

A meeting doesn't automatically mean something has gone wrong.

Office hours should be a useful collaboration channel rather than a formal escalation process.

Urgent issues should be raised immediately, not held until the next scheduled opportunity.

## When a Commitment Is at Risk

Commitments matter, but plans change. What matters is recognizing when something isn't going as expected and communicating early enough that we can do something about it.

When a commitment is at risk:

1. **Raise it early.** Don't wait until the deadline or the next weekly update.
2. **Explain what changed.** Share the impact, what you know, and what you're still figuring out.
3. **Recommend a path forward.** Consider adjusting scope, changing the timeline, or getting additional support.
4. **Align on the next commitment.** Make sure the people affected understand the revised plan, then update the existing project tracker.

Sometimes shit happens. Missing a commitment doesn't automatically mean someone failed. But we should be accountable for communicating risks, following through, and learning when things don't go as planned.

**The goal is fewer surprises and better decisions, not pretending every plan will work perfectly.**

## Weekly Project Update Template

Use this structure in the existing project tracker.

### Project: [Name]

**Week Ending:** [Date]

**DRI:** [Name]

**Status:** On Track / At Risk / Off Track

**Target Date:** [Date]

**Date Changed:** No / Yes, [what changed and why]

**Scope Changed:** No / Yes, [what changed and why]

### Progress

- [What we accomplished or learned]

### Next Steps

- [What happens next]

### Risks, Blockers, and Asks

- [Risk or blocker, impact, and help needed]

### See the Work

- [Demonstration, pull request, design, or other evidence]

This is a project reporting template. It's separate from the more personal [Weekly Update](../templates/weekly_update.md) reflection template.

## Leadership Review Questions

When reviewing an update, ask:

- Do I understand who's responsible for the outcome?
- Is the project's status clear and supported by evidence?
- Have dates or scope changed?
- Is the next meaningful action clear?
- Are important risks visible?
- Can I remove a blocker or provide useful support?
- Does this require a conversation, or can the DRI continue independently?

Avoid rewriting people's updates or turning routine project visibility into another recurring status meeting.

If communication is consistently unclear, provide coaching and improve the process.

## Responsibilities

| DRI | Leadership |
| --- | --- |
| Owns the project outcome | Establishes goals and constraints |
| Coordinates contributors and stakeholders | Provides appropriate authority and access |
| Communicates progress and risks | Reviews updates and identifies emerging concerns |
| Makes decisions within agreed boundaries | Offers context, coaching, and support |
| Raises blockers early | Removes obstacles outside the DRI's control |
| Shares results and lessons learned | Supports responsible decisions and learning |

## What Success Looks Like

This rhythm is working when:

- Everyone knows who owns a project.
- Leaders understand progress without constantly asking for updates.
- Risks and changes become visible early.
- DRIs can make decisions and collaborate directly.
- Conversations focus on solving problems rather than reporting status.
- People feel comfortable raising concerns and asking for help.
- Ownership creates opportunities for growth rather than additional bureaucracy.

The process itself should remain lightweight and adaptable.

If the weekly rhythm creates more work than value, we should reconsider how we're using it.

**Clear ownership should create more autonomy, not another layer of management.**
