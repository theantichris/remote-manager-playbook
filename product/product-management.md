# Product Management

Building great products starts with understanding the problems worth solving.

I believe Engineering, Product, and Design should work together to identify opportunities, understand customer needs, evaluate trade-offs, and deliver meaningful outcomes.

This isn't meant to prescribe how product management should work. It's how I think about approaching product development and collaborating with the people responsible for making it successful.

## Problem Discovery

Before discussing solutions, we need to understand the problems we're trying to solve.

It's easy to get excited about an idea or a feature without first understanding whether it addresses a meaningful customer need.

Some questions I like to ask:

### Understand Our Users

- What problems are our users experiencing?
- What are they trying to accomplish?
- How are they solving these problems today?
- What frustrations or limitations do they encounter?
- Are there users we're overlooking or not serving well?
- What evidence do we have that these problems are worth solving?

### Understand Our Opportunities

- How do our products compare to the alternatives?
- What strengths do we already have that we could build on?
- What can we do that our competitors can't?
- What problems are other solutions failing to address?
- What could we build today that would compound our advantage over time?

We should approach discovery with curiosity rather than trying to validate an idea we've already decided to build.

**The goal is to understand the problem before becoming attached to a solution.**

## Problem Selection

There will always be more problems worth solving than we have the capacity to address.

Choosing what not to work on is just as important as choosing what to pursue.

Once we've identified opportunities, we need to evaluate them against our current reality, strategic direction, and available resources.

### Questions to Consider

- What problems do we need to solve to succeed in our current reality?
- Where do we need to be for the next phase of our business?
- What would help us move toward that future?
- Where is the industry heading, and how can we prepare for it?
- What value would solving this problem deliver to customers?
- What is the potential return on our investment?
- What are the costs, risks, and opportunity costs?
- Are there quick wins that would deliver meaningful value?
- What could we experiment with or learn now that would make future decisions easier?

I believe we should focus on opportunities that create meaningful outcomes rather than simply keeping ourselves busy.

A smaller problem with a clear customer benefit may be more valuable than a large initiative with an uncertain outcome.

We should also consider how today's decisions affect tomorrow's opportunities. Some investments may not deliver immediate customer-facing results but can create capabilities that compound in value over time.

## Solution Validation

Once we've identified a problem worth solving, we need to determine the best way to address it.

I prefer finding inexpensive ways to test assumptions and reduce uncertainty before making significant investments.

### Questions to Consider

- What assumptions are we making about the problem and proposed solution?
- What's the biggest risk or unknown?
- How have others approached similar problems?
- Can we identify users who would benefit from the solution?
- What's the smallest experiment that would help us learn something meaningful?
- Can we validate the approach with a prototype, technical investigation, or limited release?
- What would make us reconsider or abandon the proposed solution?

We won't always have complete information, and that's okay.

I generally prefer experiments that help us learn over prolonged analysis that doesn't meaningfully reduce uncertainty.

That doesn't mean avoiding thoughtful research or planning. It means being intentional about how we spend our time learning.

### Write the Launch Announcement First

One exercise I like is writing the announcement we'd want to make after successfully delivering the solution.

Imagine explaining what we've built to our customers:

- What problem did we solve?
- Why should they care?
- How does their experience improve?
- What makes the solution valuable?
- How would we demonstrate that it worked?

If we struggle to explain why someone should be excited about the result, we may need to better understand the problem or reconsider the solution.

This is an exercise in clarifying the intended outcome, not making promises about results we haven't achieved.

### Consider the Cost of Adoption

A solution can be technically successful and still fail to deliver value.

We need to understand what adopting the solution costs our users.

That might include changes to existing workflows, learning something new, migrating data, or giving up something that already works for them.

The value we deliver should justify the effort required to adopt it.

## Working Together

I believe successful product development requires close collaboration between Engineering, Product, and Design.

Each discipline brings different expertise and perspectives.

- **Product** helps us understand customer problems, business opportunities, and priorities.
- **Design** helps us understand user experiences, workflows, and how people interact with our solutions.
- **Engineering** helps us understand technical opportunities, constraints, trade-offs, and how to build and operate sustainable solutions.

These aren't rigid boundaries. Discovery, problem-solving, and evaluating outcomes should be collaborative efforts.

Engineering shouldn't simply receive a list of features to implement. We should be involved in understanding the problems and helping determine the best solutions.

Likewise, technical feasibility shouldn't be the only consideration when deciding what to build.

**We share responsibility for delivering valuable outcomes, even when our individual responsibilities differ.**

## One-Page Product Outline

I find it helpful to capture the important details of an opportunity in a concise document before committing to a significant investment.

A useful outline includes:

### Problem

Who is affected, what problem are they experiencing, and why does it matter now?

### Desired Outcome

What will improve for our users or the business if we're successful?

### Evidence

What do we know about the problem, and what assumptions are we making?

### Constraints

What limitations do we need to consider around time, people, technology, cost, or other commitments?

### Success

How will we know we've achieved the intended outcome?

Include a baseline, target, and timeframe when meaningful.

### Possible Solutions

What approaches could address the problem? What trade-offs should we consider?

### Risks and Unknowns

What could prevent us from succeeding? What do we still need to learn?

### Experiments

What's the smallest useful thing we could do to validate our assumptions or reduce uncertainty?

This outline should help people understand the opportunity and make decisions without requiring an extensive document.

## Measuring Success

Shipping a feature isn't the same as delivering value.

After releasing a solution, we should evaluate whether it accomplished what we intended.

- Did we solve the problem?
- Did the intended users adopt the solution?
- Did their experience improve?
- Did we achieve the expected customer or business outcomes?
- What did we learn?
- What should we improve, change, or stop doing?

Sometimes the results won't match our expectations.

That's not necessarily failure. If we've learned something meaningful and use that knowledge to make better decisions, we've made progress.

**The goal isn't to build more features. It's to solve meaningful problems and deliver outcomes that matter.**
