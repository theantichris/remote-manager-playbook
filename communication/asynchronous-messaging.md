# Asynchronous Messaging

Working with a distributed team means we won't always be online at the same time. Good asynchronous communication allows us to share information, make decisions, and move work forward without requiring everyone to be available simultaneously.

The goal isn't to eliminate meetings or conversations. It's to make sure people have the context they need to do their work without unnecessary interruptions.

## Four Components of a Great Asynchronous Message

1. **Context.** Explain what's happening and why it matters. Include enough information for someone to understand the situation without having to ask a bunch of follow-up questions. Be thorough, but concise.
2. **Purpose.** Make it clear what you're communicating. Are you sharing information, asking a question, requesting feedback, or looking for a decision? If no response is needed, say so.
3. **Ownership.** Identify who needs to respond or take action. Avoid leaving everyone wondering who's responsible for the next step.
4. **Timing.** Communicate when you need a response or decision. Include a deadline and time zone when appropriate. Not everything is urgent, and not every message needs an immediate response.

## Template

Not every message needs every component, but this is a useful starting point.

- **Context:** What's going on and why does it matter?
- **Ask/Decision:** What do you need, if anything?
- **Owner(s):** Who is responsible for responding or taking action?
- **Deadline/Timezone:** When do you need a response?
- **Links/Docs:** Where can someone find additional context?

## Examples

### Good

- **Context:** We're seeing intermittent timeouts following the latest deployment. I've investigated the issue and documented two possible solutions.
- **Ask:** I'd like feedback on whether we should roll back or move forward with the proposed fix.
- **Owner(s):** Engineering DRI and Product Manager.
- **Deadline/Timezone:** I'd like a decision by 15:00 Eastern so we can address this today.
- **Links:** Investigation notes, relevant issue, and proposed fix.

This message provides the context, identifies what needs to happen, and makes it easy for the right people to respond.

### Not So Good

> Something's broken. Thoughts?

There's no context, no clear ask, and no indication of who needs to respond.

## When to Have a Conversation Instead

Asynchronous communication should be our default, but it isn't always the best approach.

Consider having a conversation when:

- **Delivering sensitive or difficult feedback.** Some conversations deserve the attention and empathy that come from speaking directly.
- **Working through complex problems.** If a long message thread isn't making progress, getting people together may be more effective.
- **Resolving disagreements or misunderstandings.** Tone and intent can get lost in writing. A conversation can help everyone reach a shared understanding.
- **Addressing urgent situations.** If something requires immediate attention, don't rely on someone eventually seeing an asynchronous message.
- **Having lengthy back-and-forth discussions.** If you're spending more time exchanging messages than solving the problem, consider a conversation.

Project kickoffs, sync-ups, and challenging problems don't automatically require meetings. Often, sharing context and gathering feedback asynchronously before a conversation makes the conversation more productive.

When we do meet, capture important decisions, action items, and relevant context in writing afterward so people who weren't there can stay informed.

**The goal is effective communication, not asynchronous communication for its own sake.**
