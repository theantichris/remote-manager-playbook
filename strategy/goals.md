# Goals

> "Bad goals are indistinguishable from numbers." — Will Larson

Goals help us understand where we want to go, focus our efforts, and make better decisions about how to get there.

I believe good goals should give teams direction and autonomy. They should help us understand what success looks like without prescribing every step required to achieve it.

A goal isn't simply a number we want to hit or a list of things we want to complete. It should represent a meaningful outcome for our customers, our teams, or the business.

**The purpose of setting goals isn't to have something to measure. It's to help us achieve something that matters.**

## What Makes a Good Goal?

Good goals generally have four important components:

1. **Target:** Where do we want to go? What outcome are we trying to achieve?
2. **Baseline:** Where are we today? What is our current level of performance?
3. **Trend:** How are things changing? Are we improving, declining, or staying the same?
4. **Time frame:** When do we expect to achieve the outcome?

Together, these help us understand the size of the challenge and whether we're making meaningful progress.

I also like two simple tests for evaluating a goal:

1. Can someone unfamiliar with the project understand how difficult the goal is?
2. Can someone unfamiliar with the project determine whether we were successful?

If the answer to either question is no, we probably need to clarify the goal.

## Outcomes Over Outputs

There's an important distinction between the work we do and the results we want to achieve.

For example:

**Output:** Ship a redesigned checkout experience.

**Outcome:** Improve the percentage of customers who successfully complete checkout.

The first describes something we're going to build. The second describes why we're building it.

Shipping something doesn't necessarily mean we've succeeded. We need to understand whether our work produced the intended result.

This distinction gives teams more freedom to determine the best approach.

If we agree on the outcome, we can experiment with different solutions, learn from the results, and adjust our approach without losing sight of what we're trying to accomplish.

## Investments and Baselines

I find it useful to think about goals in terms of **investments and baselines**.

### Investments

An investment describes something we want to improve or a future capability we want to develop.

These goals move us toward a better future.

Examples might include:

- Improving customer conversion or retention.
- Reducing the time required to deliver value.
- Increasing system capacity or performance.
- Developing capabilities that enable future products.
- Improving developer productivity.

Investment goals help us understand where we want to go and why getting there matters.

### Baselines

A baseline commitment describes something we already have that we want to maintain or protect.

These are the things we shouldn't sacrifice while pursuing improvements.

Examples might include:

- Maintaining system reliability.
- Protecting customer experience.
- Keeping operational costs within acceptable limits.
- Maintaining acceptable security and quality standards.
- Preserving the team's ability to support existing systems.

This is different from the baseline measurement used when setting a goal. A baseline measurement tells us where we are today. A baseline commitment tells us what we need to preserve.

### Balancing Investments and Baselines

I believe pairing investment goals with baseline commitments helps us make better trade-offs.

Without that balance, it's possible to achieve one goal while making something else worse.

For example, we might increase the speed at which we deliver features while introducing more defects, increasing operational costs, or creating unsustainable workloads.

A goal that improves one metric at the expense of something equally important isn't necessarily a success.

Baselines can also help us recognize when we need to change priorities.

If reliability, quality, or another important baseline begins to deteriorate, we may need to pause new investments and focus on improving the systems we already have.

**Progress shouldn't require us to sacrifice the things that make continued progress possible.**

## Example: Improving Checkout Success

Consider a hypothetical goal to improve checkout success.

### Investment Goal

Improve the weekly checkout success rate from 88% to 95% by the end of Q2.

- **Baseline:** 88% of checkout attempts succeed.
- **Trend:** Success has declined by 1.2 percentage points per month over the previous quarter.
- **Target:** 95% weekly checkout success rate.
- **Time frame:** End of Q2.

This tells us where we are, where we want to go, how things are currently changing, and when we want to achieve the outcome.

### Baseline Commitment

Maintain acceptable checkout performance while improving the success rate.

For example:

- Current 95th-percentile checkout response time: 1.2 seconds.
- Acceptable threshold: 1.5 seconds.

This gives us an important constraint.

We want more customers to complete checkout successfully, but not by introducing unacceptable performance problems.

### What This Enables

The team now understands both the desired improvement and the constraints they need to consider.

Rather than prescribing a solution, we can explore different approaches:

- Investigate the causes of checkout failures.
- Improve error handling.
- Address performance bottlenecks.
- Simplify confusing user interactions.
- Experiment with changes and measure their impact.

The goal provides direction while leaving the team room to determine how best to achieve it.

## Using Goals to Make Decisions

Goals should be useful throughout the work, not just when planning begins or a quarter ends.

When we're making decisions, I want us to consider:

- Does this work move us closer to an important outcome?
- What evidence supports our approach?
- What trade-offs are we making?
- Are we protecting the baselines that matter?
- Have we learned something that should change our approach?
- Is this still the most important thing we could be working on?

We should review progress regularly, paying attention to trends and evidence rather than simply reporting whether something is on track.

Sometimes we'll discover that our original assumptions were wrong. When that happens, we should be willing to adjust our plans or reconsider the goal itself.

I don't want teams chasing a metric simply because we committed to it months ago.

Likewise, I don't want us changing targets just to declare success.

**The goal is to make informed decisions, learn, and deliver meaningful outcomes.**

## What Success Looks Like

A good goal helps everyone understand what we're trying to accomplish and why it matters.

It provides enough clarity for people to make decisions independently while establishing the boundaries and trade-offs we need to consider.

We should be able to evaluate our progress, recognize when something isn't working, and adjust our approach based on what we learn.

Most importantly, achieving a goal should mean we've made something meaningfully better.

**Goals are valuable when they help us make better decisions, not when they simply give us more numbers to report.**
