# Strategy and Vision

Vision and strategy help us understand where we're going, why it matters, and how we intend to get there.

I believe a clear vision and a good strategy are essential for giving teams the context and autonomy they need to make good decisions.

Without them, teams can end up working toward different outcomes, making conflicting decisions, or delivering things that don't contribute to a larger purpose.

**Vision describes the future we want to create. Strategy explains how we intend to get there.**

Neither should be a document we write once and forget about. They should help guide our decisions and evolve as we learn.

## Vision

A vision describes a future we're working toward.

It should help people understand what we're trying to accomplish, why it's important, and what the world will look like if we're successful.

I believe a good vision helps people who don't work closely together make decisions that move us in the same direction.

It shouldn't prescribe every step along the way. Instead, it should provide enough clarity that teams can independently determine how their work contributes to the bigger picture.

### Vision Statement

A vision statement should be a short, aspirational description of the future we want to create.

Ideally, it can be communicated in one or two sentences and is easy for people to understand and remember.

It should be something we can refer to during planning, decision-making, and conversations about priorities.

### Value Proposition

What value will we provide to our customers, users, or organization?

Consider:

- Who are we trying to help?
- What problems will we solve?
- How will their experience improve?
- What will we make possible that isn't possible today?
- Why will this future be meaningfully better?

A vision should describe the value we're trying to create, not simply a collection of features or capabilities we want to build.

### Future Capabilities

What capabilities will we need to make this vision possible?

These might include new technology, improved processes, different ways of collaborating, or skills we need to develop.

Focus on the capabilities we need rather than prematurely deciding exactly how we'll build them.

### Constraints

Think about the constraints that exist today and how they might change.

- **Solved constraints:** What limitations do we expect to overcome?
- **Future constraints:** What new challenges might emerge as we grow or succeed?

A compelling vision should recognize that solving today's problems doesn't mean eliminating every future challenge.

### Narrative

Bring the vision together into a story people can understand.

Describe what the future looks like, how it differs from today, and why that difference matters.

The goal isn't to create an elaborate presentation. It's to give people a shared understanding of the future we're working toward.

## Strategy

A strategy describes how we intend to make progress toward our vision given the challenges, opportunities, and constraints we face today.

I think of strategy as a set of deliberate choices.

We can't pursue every opportunity, solve every problem, or invest in everything we'd like to improve. Our resources and attention are limited.

Good strategy helps us decide where to focus, what trade-offs we're willing to make, and what we aren't going to pursue.

Richard Rumelt describes three essential elements of good strategy in *Good Strategy/Bad Strategy*:

1. Diagnosis
2. Guiding Policy
3. Coherent Actions

I find this framework useful because it forces us to move beyond vague ambitions and identify how we're actually going to address a challenge.

### Diagnosis

A diagnosis explains the challenge we're trying to overcome.

It should describe the current situation, the underlying problem, and the constraints or conditions that make it difficult.

Some questions to consider:

- What problem are we trying to solve?
- Why does this problem exist?
- What evidence supports our understanding?
- What constraints are we operating within?
- What opportunities or advantages do we have?
- What happens if we do nothing?

I believe understanding the problem is one of the most important parts of developing a strategy.

If our diagnosis is wrong, even well-executed actions may fail to produce the outcome we want.

**Before deciding what we're going to do, we need to understand why we're doing it.**

### Guiding Policy

A guiding policy describes the approach we'll take to address the challenge.

It establishes the principles and trade-offs that should guide our decisions.

This isn't a detailed implementation plan. It's a way of narrowing the available options so we can focus our efforts.

Some questions to consider:

- Where are we going to focus?
- What advantages can we build on?
- What constraints must we respect?
- What trade-offs are we willing to make?
- What approaches are we deliberately avoiding?
- How will we know whether a proposed action fits our strategy?

A good guiding policy gives teams enough direction to make independent decisions without requiring leadership to approve every detail.

### Coherent Actions

Coherent actions describe what we're going to do to put our guiding policy into practice.

These actions should reinforce each other and collectively address the challenge identified in our diagnosis.

A strategy isn't simply a list of projects or initiatives.

We should be able to explain how each action contributes to the larger approach and why we're investing in it.

Some questions to consider:

- What actions will have the greatest impact?
- How do those actions support each other?
- What dependencies exist?
- Who is responsible for the outcomes?
- What needs to happen first?
- What will we stop doing to make room for this work?

Each action should contribute to a coherent approach rather than compete with the others for attention and resources.

## Trade-offs and Anti-Goals

I believe one of the most important parts of strategy is being clear about what we're choosing not to do.

If everything is a priority, nothing is.

Trade-offs help us focus our resources and make decisions when multiple opportunities compete for our attention.

### Out of Scope

What are we explicitly not addressing with this strategy?

Being clear about scope helps us avoid unnecessary expansion and keeps our efforts focused.

### Anti-Goals

What outcomes or approaches are we intentionally avoiding?

Anti-goals can help us establish boundaries and prevent decisions that might appear beneficial in isolation but conflict with our larger objectives.

For example, improving development speed shouldn't require sacrificing system reliability or creating unsustainable workloads.

### Risks

What could go wrong?

Consider the assumptions we're making, dependencies we rely on, and circumstances that could prevent us from succeeding.

We don't need to eliminate every risk before moving forward, but we should understand the important ones and determine how we'll respond.

## Strategy Should Enable Autonomy

One of the greatest benefits of a clear strategy is that people can make decisions without constantly seeking direction.

When teams understand the vision, the problems we're solving, and the trade-offs we're willing to make, they have the context needed to make good decisions independently.

I don't want to be involved in every decision. I want to help establish enough clarity that people feel confident making those decisions themselves.

That means:

- Communicating the vision and strategy clearly.
- Establishing ownership and decision boundaries.
- Explaining the reasons behind our choices.
- Making important constraints and trade-offs visible.
- Giving people the authority and support to act.
- Encouraging people to raise questions when new information challenges our assumptions.

**Good strategy should reduce the number of decisions that need to be escalated, not create another layer of approval.**

## Example: Improving Software Delivery

Consider an engineering organization that wants teams to deliver customer value more quickly without compromising reliability.

### Vision

Teams can deliver valuable changes safely and independently, without unnecessary delays or relying on a small group of specialists.

### Diagnosis

Delivery is slow because deployments require manual coordination, release processes are inconsistent, and teams depend on a small number of people to resolve problems.

These dependencies create bottlenecks and make frequent releases difficult.

### Guiding Policy

Invest in standardized, self-service delivery capabilities that reduce dependencies while maintaining reliability.

Prefer solutions that teams can adopt independently and that reduce operational complexity over time.

### Coherent Actions

- Standardize deployment processes.
- Introduce automated quality and safety checks.
- Improve observability and rollback capabilities.
- Reduce manual approval requirements where appropriate.
- Provide documentation and tooling that allow teams to manage their own releases.

### Trade-offs

We may choose to delay lower-priority feature work to invest in delivery infrastructure.

We may also accept some short-term complexity while transitioning away from inconsistent deployment processes.

### Measuring Success

We might evaluate progress through reduced lead time for changes, fewer manual dependencies, and improved developer experience.

We would also establish reliability expectations to ensure that improving delivery speed doesn't introduce unacceptable operational risk.

The strategy provides a clear direction without prescribing every technical decision.

Teams can determine the best implementation approach while understanding the outcomes they're expected to achieve.

## Connecting Strategy to Goals

A strategy explains how we intend to address a challenge. Goals help us determine whether we're making meaningful progress.

We should connect our strategic actions to measurable outcomes whenever possible.

I find it useful to pair investment goals with baseline commitments so we can pursue improvements without sacrificing the things that already work.

See [Goals](goals.md) for more on this approach.

Shipping the projects described in our strategy isn't necessarily success. We need to understand whether those investments actually moved us toward our intended outcomes.

## Revisiting Strategy

Strategies are based on our understanding of the world at a particular point in time.

That understanding will change.

We'll learn new things, encounter unexpected constraints, discover opportunities, and sometimes realize our original assumptions were wrong.

I don't believe we should continue following a strategy simply because we committed to it.

We should revisit it when meaningful new information becomes available.

Some questions to ask:

- Is our diagnosis still accurate?
- Are our assumptions holding up?
- Are our actions producing the intended outcomes?
- Have important constraints or opportunities changed?
- Are we making the trade-offs we intended?
- Is this still the best approach to achieving our vision?

Being willing to change our strategy doesn't mean we lack direction.

It means we're using what we've learned to make better decisions.

At the same time, we shouldn't change direction every time something becomes difficult. We need to distinguish between a strategy that isn't working and one that simply requires patience and persistence.

## What Success Looks Like

A good vision gives people a shared understanding of the future we're trying to create.

A good strategy helps us understand the challenges we face, the choices we're making, and the actions we'll take to move toward that future.

Together, they should help people:

- Understand why their work matters.
- Make decisions that align with our direction.
- Recognize important trade-offs and constraints.
- Focus on meaningful outcomes.
- Collaborate without unnecessary coordination.
- Adapt as we learn.

**The value of a strategy isn't in the document itself. It's in the clarity, alignment, and better decisions it enables.**
