# Competencies

Engineering growth is about more than writing code, completing tickets, or earning a new title. It's about developing your skills, increasing your impact, and becoming more effective at helping your team and organization succeed.

I find it useful to think about professional growth across three areas:

- **Experience:** Your technical expertise, judgment, problem-solving abilities, and how you apply what you've learned.
- **Impact:** The value your work creates for customers, teammates, and the organization.
- **Leadership:** Your ability to take ownership, influence decisions, help others succeed, and move things forward.

These competencies are connected, but they don't always develop at the same pace. Someone may have deep technical expertise while still developing their ability to influence others. Another person may demonstrate strong leadership while continuing to build technical depth.

That's normal. Growth looks different for everyone.

## Experience

Experience is about developing expertise in your craft and applying it effectively.

It isn't simply the number of years you've worked, the amount of code you've written, or how quickly you complete tasks.

I value good judgment, thoughtful problem-solving, quality, and the ability to learn from experience.

### Developing

- Completes well-defined work with appropriate guidance and support.
- Builds technical knowledge and learns established practices.
- Asks questions and seeks feedback when encountering unfamiliar problems.
- Understands the importance of testing, maintainability, and quality.
- Recognizes when they need help and communicates challenges early.

### Established

- Independently solves increasingly complex or ambiguous problems.
- Makes thoughtful technical decisions and understands their trade-offs.
- Produces reliable, maintainable solutions.
- Shares knowledge and helps others develop their technical skills.
- Identifies opportunities to improve engineering practices and systems.
- Applies lessons learned from previous experiences to new challenges.

### Leading

- Helps establish technical practices and approaches that others adopt.
- Navigates complex technical challenges involving multiple systems or teams.
- Anticipates risks and considers the long-term consequences of decisions.
- Balances immediate delivery needs with sustainability and technical quality.
- Develops the technical capabilities of others through mentoring, collaboration, and example.
- Helps the organization make better technical decisions.

## Impact

Impact is the value your work creates.

I don't believe individual productivity or the amount of work completed tells us everything about someone's contribution.

Helping another engineer succeed, simplifying a complicated process, preventing a production incident, or improving a system that many teams depend on can create significant value.

Impact should be considered in the context of someone's responsibilities, opportunities, and the problems they're working to solve.

### Developing

- Delivers meaningful contributions to team goals.
- Understands how their work connects to larger outcomes.
- Follows through on commitments and communicates progress.
- Identifies opportunities to improve the work directly within their responsibility.
- Contributes to team success through reliable execution and collaboration.

### Established

- Delivers outcomes that meaningfully improve the team's effectiveness or customer experience.
- Identifies and addresses problems beyond their immediate assignments.
- Helps remove obstacles that affect other team members.
- Improves systems, processes, or practices that benefit the team.
- Makes thoughtful trade-offs between short-term needs and long-term value.
- Recognizes opportunities where their expertise can create greater impact.

### Leading

- Creates meaningful improvements across teams or the organization.
- Identifies high-leverage opportunities that contribute to strategic goals.
- Helps others become more effective, multiplying their collective impact.
- Addresses systemic problems rather than repeatedly treating symptoms.
- Connects technical decisions and investments to customer and business outcomes.
- Creates improvements that continue delivering value beyond their direct involvement.

## Leadership

Leadership isn't limited to people managers or individuals with senior titles.

Anyone can demonstrate leadership through ownership, sound judgment, collaboration, and helping others succeed.

I believe leadership is about influence and responsibility, not authority.

As people grow, they should develop the confidence and ability to make decisions, navigate uncertainty, and help others move toward shared goals.

### Developing

- Takes ownership of their responsibilities and follows through on commitments.
- Communicates progress, challenges, and risks.
- Participates constructively in team discussions and decisions.
- Offers ideas and feedback.
- Asks for help when needed and supports teammates when possible.
- Demonstrates accountability for their work and decisions.

### Established

- Leads projects or initiatives within their area of responsibility.
- Makes decisions independently within established boundaries.
- Helps teammates navigate challenges and develop their skills.
- Communicates effectively with stakeholders and partners.
- Builds alignment around goals, priorities, and technical decisions.
- Handles disagreements constructively and considers different perspectives.
- Identifies problems and takes initiative to address them.

### Leading

- Provides direction and clarity for complex or cross-team initiatives.
- Influences decisions through expertise, judgment, and trust rather than authority.
- Helps establish strategies and approaches that others can act on independently.
- Develops future leaders through mentoring, coaching, and creating opportunities.
- Navigates competing priorities and organizational trade-offs.
- Builds relationships and alignment across teams.
- Creates an environment where others can take ownership and succeed.

## Understanding Development Stages

Developing, Established, and Leading describe patterns of growth, not job titles, tenure requirements, or formal performance ratings.

They aren't intended to replace an organization's career ladder or competency framework.

Someone can demonstrate behaviors from multiple stages depending on the situation.

For example, an engineer might demonstrate leading technical expertise while continuing to develop cross-functional leadership skills.

That's not a contradiction. It's an opportunity for growth.

I also don't believe everyone needs to reach the Leading stage in every competency.

Some people want to deepen their technical expertise. Others want to expand their organizational influence, mentor others, or pursue management.

What matters is understanding your strengths, interests, and the opportunities available to you.

## Evaluating Competencies

Competencies should be evaluated through observable behaviors and meaningful outcomes rather than assumptions or arbitrary measurements.

Some useful sources of evidence include:

- Projects and outcomes you've contributed to.
- Technical decisions and the trade-offs you've navigated.
- Improvements you've made to systems or processes.
- Feedback from teammates and collaborators.
- Examples of mentoring, coaching, or helping others succeed.
- Challenges you've navigated and what you've learned.
- Responsibilities you've taken on and how you've handled them.

Context matters.

Someone shouldn't be considered less capable simply because they haven't been given the opportunity to demonstrate a particular behavior.

Likewise, an isolated example shouldn't automatically define someone's overall capability.

I want to look for consistent patterns, recognize demonstrated strengths, and identify opportunities to grow.

## Using Competencies for Career Development

I see competencies primarily as a tool for meaningful career development conversations.

They can help us understand:

- Where are your strengths?
- What kind of impact are you having?
- What skills or behaviors would you like to develop?
- What opportunities could help you practice those skills?
- What support do you need from me?
- How will we recognize progress?

We'll use these conversations to inform your [Career Development Plan](../templates/career-development-plan.md) and identify opportunities that align with your interests and goals.

I don't want this to become a checklist where someone feels obligated to demonstrate every behavior.

The goal is to understand how you're growing and how I can help you continue developing.

## What Success Looks Like

A useful competency framework should help people recognize their strengths, understand opportunities for improvement, and make intentional decisions about their development.

It should encourage meaningful conversations rather than reduce people to scores or labels.

Most importantly, it should recognize that valuable engineering contributions take many forms.

**Growth isn't about checking every box or reaching the next title. It's about becoming more effective at the work you want to do and increasing the positive impact you have on the people around you.**
