# Team States

Engineering teams don't always operate at the same level of effectiveness.

Sometimes we're struggling to keep up with existing responsibilities. Sometimes we're making progress but don't have the capacity to improve things. Other times, we're able to invest in the systems, capabilities, and ideas that will help us succeed in the future.

I find it useful to think about team health through four states:

1. Falling Behind
2. Treading Water
3. Repaying Debt
4. Innovating

These states help us understand how a team is operating, what challenges it faces, and where we should focus our efforts.

**The goal isn't to label teams as good or bad. It's to understand what they need to become more effective and sustainable.**

## Understanding Team States

I don't believe these states are necessarily a linear progression or a measure of team maturity.

A team might be innovating in one area while falling behind in another. A team that's operating effectively today might struggle tomorrow because its responsibilities, priorities, or circumstances change.

That's normal.

We should use these states as a way to start conversations, identify problems, and make better decisions rather than assigning teams a permanent classification.

It's also important to recognize that a team's operating state isn't necessarily a reflection of the abilities or efforts of the people on it.

A talented, hardworking team can struggle because of unclear priorities, excessive commitments, technical debt, dependencies, or organizational constraints.

As leaders, our responsibility is to understand those factors and help address them.

## Falling Behind

### What It Looks Like

The team is struggling to keep up with its responsibilities.

New work arrives faster than existing work can be completed. The backlog grows, priorities constantly shift, and people spend more time reacting to problems than making meaningful progress.

Some signals might include:

- The backlog continues to grow despite everyone working hard.
- Important commitments are frequently missed or delayed.
- Production incidents and operational interruptions consume significant capacity.
- Work in progress increases while completed work remains relatively flat.
- Team members feel overwhelmed or frustrated.
- Customers or stakeholders are increasingly dissatisfied.
- People struggle to find time for improvement or learning.

The team may be working incredibly hard without seeing meaningful results.

### Understanding the Problem

My first instinct shouldn't be to tell people to work harder or immediately hire more engineers.

We need to understand why the team is falling behind.

Some questions I like to ask:

- Are we taking on more work than we have the capacity to complete?
- Are our priorities clear?
- Are we constantly interrupting work with new requests?
- Is technical debt making seemingly simple changes difficult?
- Are dependencies preventing the team from making progress?
- Do we have the skills and resources necessary to meet our responsibilities?
- Are we spending time on work that doesn't meaningfully contribute to our goals?

Adding more people might help if the problem is genuinely insufficient capacity. But it won't necessarily solve unclear priorities, excessive work in progress, or ineffective processes.

### What Helps

- **Reduce work in progress.** Stop starting new things and focus on finishing what's already important.
- **Clarify priorities.** Work with stakeholders to understand what must happen now and what can wait.
- **Reduce interruptions.** Protect enough focused time for the team to make meaningful progress.
- **Address systemic blockers.** Identify and remove dependencies, process problems, and recurring sources of friction.
- **Set realistic expectations.** Be transparent with stakeholders about capacity, trade-offs, and what can reasonably be accomplished.
- **Create opportunities for progress.** Identify meaningful, achievable outcomes that help rebuild momentum and confidence.
- **Consider additional capacity.** When the underlying problem truly requires more people or different expertise, make that investment intentionally.

I want us to focus on creating conditions where the team can succeed rather than expecting people to compensate for systemic problems through unsustainable effort.

## Treading Water

### What It Looks Like

The team is keeping up with its most important responsibilities but has little capacity for anything else.

Critical work gets completed, production systems remain operational, and stakeholders receive the support they need.

However, the team struggles to make meaningful progress on technical debt, developer experience, or larger improvements.

Some signals might include:

- Important commitments are generally met.
- Operational responsibilities consume most available capacity.
- Technical debt remains relatively unchanged or slowly accumulates.
- Larger initiatives are difficult to start or complete.
- Improvements are frequently postponed to make room for immediate needs.
- The team feels productive but struggles to make lasting progress.

This is a more sustainable position than constantly falling behind, but it can become a trap.

If we're always using our available capacity to maintain the status quo, we aren't creating the conditions necessary to improve it.

### Understanding the Problem

Some questions worth exploring:

- Are we spending too much time maintaining existing systems?
- What recurring problems consume our capacity?
- Are we prioritizing immediate requests over longer-term improvements?
- Could we eliminate or automate repetitive work?
- Are we committing to too much required work?
- What investments could create more capacity in the future?

### What Helps

- **Continue limiting WIP.** Protect the team's ability to finish work rather than starting more.
- **Identify recurring costs.** Look for repeated incidents, manual processes, and maintenance tasks that consume capacity.
- **Make deliberate investments.** Reserve time for improvements that reduce future work.
- **Challenge existing commitments.** Make sure we're still working on the most valuable things.
- **Focus on team outcomes.** Encourage people to optimize for collective progress rather than individual busyness.
- **Create some breathing room.** Avoid committing every available hour to required work.

This connects to my approach to [Pants Based Sizing](../process/sizing.md).

If we fill our plates entirely with required work, we'll never have room for cake.

Sometimes a small investment today can create significantly more capacity tomorrow.

## Repaying Debt

### What It Looks Like

The team has enough stability and capacity to begin making meaningful improvements to its systems and ways of working.

Instead of constantly reacting to problems, people can address the underlying causes.

Technical debt begins to decrease, recurring problems become less frequent, and the team starts reclaiming time previously consumed by maintenance and interruptions.

Some signals might include:

- Recurring incidents or operational problems become less frequent.
- Changes become easier and safer to make.
- Manual processes are reduced or automated.
- Developers spend less time working around existing problems.
- Lead times become more predictable.
- The team can invest in improvements without sacrificing critical commitments.
- People have more opportunities to improve systems and practices.

### Understanding the Opportunity

Technical debt isn't inherently bad. Sometimes taking on debt is a reasonable trade-off to achieve an important outcome.

What matters is understanding the cost of carrying that debt and making intentional decisions about addressing it.

Some questions I like to ask:

- What technical debt creates the greatest ongoing cost?
- Which improvements would have the most meaningful impact?
- Are there recurring problems we could eliminate rather than repeatedly address?
- What investments would make future work easier?
- How can we demonstrate the value of these improvements?
- Are we addressing underlying problems or just making symptoms less painful?

### What Helps

- **Prioritize high-impact improvements.** Focus on debt that affects reliability, delivery, quality, or the team's ability to make progress.
- **Protect improvement capacity.** Don't immediately fill newly available time with additional commitments.
- **Make improvements incrementally.** Avoid turning every technical improvement into an enormous project.
- **Measure outcomes.** Look for improvements in reliability, lead time, operational workload, and developer experience.
- **Keep delivering value.** Balance technical improvements with customer and business needs.
- **Allow benefits to compound.** Use the capacity created by previous improvements to make additional investments.

I particularly like the compounding nature of this state.

As we address problems that consume our time, we create additional capacity to address other problems.

That cycle can significantly improve a team's effectiveness over time.

**Repaying technical debt shouldn't be something we do only when there's nothing else to work on. It should be part of maintaining a healthy engineering organization.**

## Innovating

### What It Looks Like

The team has enough stability, autonomy, and capacity to explore opportunities and deliver new value without constantly struggling against existing problems.

This doesn't mean technical debt has disappeared or that everything is perfect.

It means the team's existing responsibilities are manageable enough that it can make deliberate investments in the future.

Some signals might include:

- The team consistently delivers meaningful outcomes.
- Operational responsibilities are manageable and predictable.
- Technical debt is understood and addressed sustainably.
- People have opportunities to experiment, learn, and improve.
- Teams can make decisions without excessive coordination or approval.
- Stakeholders have confidence in the team's ability to deliver.
- Morale is healthy and people feel ownership of their work.
- The team has capacity to respond to unexpected challenges.

### Understanding the Opportunity

Innovation isn't limited to creating new products or features.

It can include improving existing systems, exploring new technologies, simplifying processes, or discovering better ways to solve problems.

Some questions worth exploring:

- What opportunities could create meaningful customer or business value?
- What could we experiment with or learn?
- What capabilities would help us succeed in the future?
- Where could we simplify or improve existing solutions?
- What assumptions should we challenge?
- How can we continue improving without compromising the health of our existing systems?

### What Helps

- **Maintain slack.** Don't respond to increased effectiveness by filling every available moment with additional work.
- **Encourage experimentation.** Give people opportunities to explore ideas and learn from the results.
- **Protect autonomy.** Let teams make decisions within clear boundaries.
- **Continue investing in quality.** Avoid allowing technical debt and operational problems to accumulate again.
- **Focus on meaningful outcomes.** Innovation should create value, not just produce new technology or features.
- **Recognize and share learning.** Encourage teams to share discoveries, including experiments that didn't produce the expected results.
- **Maintain sustainable expectations.** Protect the conditions that made innovation possible.

I believe this is where we want teams to spend as much of their time as reasonably possible.

Not because innovation is inherently more valuable than maintaining existing systems, but because healthy systems and effective practices give us the freedom to pursue new opportunities.

**Innovation should be a sustainable part of how we operate, not something we occasionally find time for.**

## Recognizing Team Health

No single metric can tell us whether a team is healthy or effective.

I prefer looking at a combination of quantitative signals and the experiences of the people doing the work.

Some useful indicators include:

| Area | Signals |
| --- | --- |
| Delivery | Lead time, throughput trends, predictability, and work in progress |
| Reliability | Incident frequency, severity, recovery time, and operational load |
| Quality | Defects, rework, and recurring technical problems |
| Sustainability | Workload, interruptions, on-call burden, and available capacity |
| Team Experience | Morale, engagement, collaboration, and psychological safety |
| Customer Impact | Satisfaction, adoption, support issues, and meaningful product outcomes |

These metrics need context.

Increased throughput doesn't necessarily mean the team is more effective. Reduced incident counts don't tell us much if we're no longer detecting problems. High delivery activity doesn't guarantee we're creating value.

We should use metrics to help us ask better questions rather than make assumptions about people or teams.

## Responsibilities

Improving a team's operating state requires collaboration.

### Managers

- Identify and help remove systemic obstacles.
- Establish clear priorities and expectations.
- Protect the team's capacity and focus.
- Work with stakeholders to manage commitments and trade-offs.
- Ensure people have the coaching, resources, and support they need.
- Create an environment where problems can be raised without fear of blame.

### Team Members

- Take ownership of outcomes and commitments.
- Communicate risks and blockers early.
- Limit work in progress and focus on finishing meaningful work.
- Identify opportunities to improve systems and practices.
- Collaborate and share knowledge.
- Help maintain the quality and reliability of the systems they own.

### Partners and Stakeholders

- Align on priorities and intended outcomes.
- Make trade-offs visible.
- Respect the team's capacity and existing commitments.
- Collaborate on scope and timing.
- Help reduce unnecessary dependencies and coordination overhead.

No single person can make a team effective through effort alone.

We need to create conditions where everyone can contribute to sustainable improvement.

## Moving Between States

I don't believe teams should be evaluated by how quickly they move from one state to another.

The states are a tool for understanding our current circumstances, not a ladder we're expected to climb.

We should regularly ask:

- What state best describes how we're operating today?
- Are different parts of our responsibilities in different states?
- What's helping us succeed?
- What's getting in our way?
- What meaningful change could improve our situation?
- What should we stop doing?
- What investment would create the greatest long-term benefit?

Sometimes the right decision is to invest in reliability rather than pursue something new.

Sometimes we need to reduce commitments, change priorities, improve processes, or add capacity.

We should make those decisions based on evidence and the outcomes we're trying to achieve.

This also connects to the distinction between investments and baseline commitments in [Goals](../strategy/goals.md).

We want to improve the future without sacrificing the things we need to maintain today.

## What Success Looks Like

A healthy engineering team can deliver meaningful value, maintain the systems it owns, and continuously improve how it works.

People have clear priorities, manageable responsibilities, and opportunities to grow.

The team has enough capacity to respond to unexpected challenges without constantly sacrificing its longer-term goals.

Leadership understands the team's challenges and helps address the underlying problems rather than simply asking people to do more.

Most importantly, the team can sustain its effectiveness over time.

**The goal isn't to reach a particular state. It's to create an environment where teams can consistently do meaningful work, improve their systems, and have the capacity to build what's next.**
