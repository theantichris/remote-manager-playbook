# Team Sizing

The size and composition of a team have a significant impact on its ability to collaborate, deliver value, and grow.

I believe teams should be large enough to accomplish meaningful work independently, but small enough to maintain strong relationships, clear ownership, and effective communication.

There's no perfect team size for every situation. The goal isn't to reach a particular number. It's to create teams that can work effectively and sustainably.

## Preferred Team Size

I generally prefer engineering teams of **six to eight people** during steady-state operations.

I've found this to be a useful balance between having enough people to accomplish meaningful work and keeping the team small enough to collaborate effectively.

Teams in this range can often:

- Maintain clear ownership and accountability.
- Build strong relationships and trust.
- Collaborate without excessive coordination.
- Distribute responsibilities without creating unnecessary dependencies.
- Support each other's development through mentoring and knowledge sharing.
- Balance delivering new capabilities with maintaining existing systems.

This is a guideline, not a requirement.

The right size depends on the complexity of the work, the experience of the people involved, and the responsibilities the team owns.

### Team Size vs. Management Span

It's important to distinguish between the size of an engineering team and the number of direct reports a manager supports.

A cross-functional team may include engineers, Product, Design, and other partners who don't report to the same manager.

Similarly, a manager may support people working across multiple initiatives or teams.

I believe a manager's span of control should allow enough time for meaningful coaching, career development, strategic thinking, and removing obstacles.

As the number of direct reports increases, it becomes harder to provide everyone with the attention and support they deserve.

The complexity of the team and the support each person needs matter just as much as the number of people reporting to a manager.

## Smaller Teams

Smaller teams can be effective, particularly when exploring new opportunities, developing early capabilities, or working on narrowly scoped problems.

They often benefit from simpler coordination and the ability to make decisions quickly.

However, smaller teams can also face challenges:

- Limited opportunities to distribute responsibilities.
- Greater dependence on individual contributors.
- Difficulty covering specialized skills or operational responsibilities.
- Less flexibility when people are unavailable.
- Managers becoming heavily involved in day-to-day technical work.

None of these challenges mean a small team can't succeed.

We should understand what the team needs to accomplish and make sure it has the resources and support to do so.

## Larger Teams

As teams grow, coordination becomes more complicated.

More people can increase our capacity, but they also introduce additional communication paths, dependencies, and management responsibilities.

Some warning signs that a team may be getting too large include:

- People losing visibility into what others are working on.
- Increasing coordination overhead.
- Ownership and responsibilities becoming unclear.
- Decisions requiring more people than necessary.
- Managers struggling to provide meaningful coaching and feedback.
- Team members having fewer opportunities to take ownership or influence decisions.

Adding more people doesn't automatically mean we'll accomplish more.

We should consider whether increasing the size of a team will actually improve its ability to deliver meaningful outcomes.

## When to Split a Team

I generally prefer growing an established team to around **eight to ten people before considering a split**.

This gives us an opportunity to develop enough capacity and shared experience to form two sustainable teams.

However, reaching a particular number shouldn't automatically trigger a reorganization.

Before splitting a team, I want us to consider:

- Can we identify meaningful, independent areas of ownership?
- Will each resulting team have a clear mission and purpose?
- Do both teams have the skills and experience needed to succeed?
- Can they make decisions and deliver value without excessive dependencies?
- Do we have the leadership capacity to support both teams?
- Will the split improve focus, ownership, and effectiveness?

We shouldn't split a team simply to make an organizational chart look better.

A poorly planned split can create new dependencies, increase coordination overhead, and make it harder for people to accomplish their work.

**The goal is to create two effective teams, not simply divide one team into two groups.**

## Team Composition

Team size isn't the only consideration. The skills, experience, and responsibilities represented within the team matter just as much.

I want teams to have the capabilities necessary to own their work and deliver meaningful outcomes.

Depending on the team, this may include:

- Technical expertise across the systems they own.
- Experience with architecture and technical decision-making.
- Quality and testing capabilities.
- Operational knowledge, observability, and reliability.
- Product and Design collaboration.
- A healthy mix of experience levels.
- People who can mentor, coach, and help others grow.

Not every capability needs to be represented by a dedicated person on the team.

What's important is that the team has reliable access to the expertise and support it needs without creating unnecessary dependencies.

## Keep Innovation and Maintenance Together

I believe teams should generally maintain ownership of the systems and capabilities they build.

Separating innovation from maintenance can create problems:

- Teams responsible for building new features may not experience the long-term consequences of their decisions.
- Maintenance teams can become disconnected from the product decisions that created the systems they support.
- Handoffs introduce coordination overhead and opportunities for misunderstanding.
- Ownership becomes fragmented.
- Maintaining existing systems can be viewed as less valuable than building new ones.

I prefer teams that can both build new capabilities and take responsibility for their ongoing operation, quality, and improvement.

This helps create accountability and encourages better long-term technical decisions.

It also gives engineers opportunities to understand the full lifecycle of the systems they work on.

There are situations where specialized teams or shared platform capabilities make sense. However, those should enable product teams rather than remove their responsibility for the outcomes and systems they own.

**The people building a system should understand what it takes to keep that system healthy.**

## Exceptions

Not every team needs to follow the same structure.

### Incubation and Exploration

Small teams can be useful when we're exploring new ideas, validating assumptions, or developing early capabilities.

These teams may prioritize learning and speed over having every capability represented internally.

As the work matures, we should revisit whether the team has the right size and composition for sustained ownership.

### Temporary Cross-Team Initiatives

Sometimes we need people from several teams to collaborate on a specific problem or opportunity.

These groups may be larger or have a different structure than our typical engineering teams.

I want us to establish clear ownership, intended outcomes, and expectations about how long the collaboration will last.

Temporary groups shouldn't create permanent organizational complexity without a good reason.

### Specialized Teams

Some teams require highly specialized skills or own capabilities that serve multiple other teams.

Their size and composition should reflect those responsibilities rather than conform to a preferred number.

The important thing is understanding their mission, dependencies, and how they enable the broader organization.

## Reassessing Team Size

Team structures shouldn't remain unchanged simply because they've worked in the past.

As responsibilities, systems, and people evolve, we should periodically reconsider whether our teams are organized effectively.

Some questions I like to ask:

- Does the team have a clear mission and ownership?
- Can the team deliver meaningful outcomes independently?
- Is collaboration effective, or are we spending too much time coordinating?
- Are people getting the coaching and support they need?
- Is the workload sustainable?
- Do we have the right mix of skills and experience?
- Are dependencies preventing us from making progress?
- Would changing the team's size or structure meaningfully improve its effectiveness?

The answers should help us determine whether to grow, split, consolidate, or maintain the team as it is.

We should also consider whether a problem is actually caused by team size.

Adding people won't necessarily solve unclear priorities, excessive work in progress, poor processes, or organizational dependencies.

## What Success Looks Like

An effective team has the people, capabilities, and support needed to accomplish its mission.

Team members understand their responsibilities, trust each other, collaborate effectively, and have opportunities to grow.

Managers have enough capacity to invest in their people and help the team succeed.

The organization benefits from clear ownership, sustainable delivery, and teams that can make decisions without unnecessary coordination.

**The goal isn't to build teams of a particular size. It's to build teams that can do great work together.**
