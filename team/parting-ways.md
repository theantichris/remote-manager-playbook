# Parting Ways

Ending someone's employment is one of the most difficult responsibilities we have as managers.

I believe it's important to remember that firing someone isn't nearly as unpleasant as being fired. What may be a difficult conversation for us can be a life-changing event for the person on the other side.

Our responsibility is to handle that experience with sensitivity, compassion, honesty, and respect.

We can't eliminate the pain or uncertainty someone may experience, but we should do everything reasonably possible to avoid making an already difficult situation worse.

**The goal isn't to make the conversation easier for ourselves. It's to treat the person with dignity throughout the process.**

## Principles

A few things I believe are important when parting ways with someone:

- **Lead with compassion.** Remember that we're talking to a person whose livelihood, relationships, and sense of security may be affected.
- **Be honest and direct.** Don't create confusion or false hope by avoiding difficult information.
- **Treat people with dignity.** Someone's employment ending doesn't diminish their worth as a person or the contributions they've made.
- **Reduce unnecessary uncertainty.** Be prepared to explain what happens next and connect the person with the information and support they need.
- **Respect their privacy.** Share sensitive information only with people who have a legitimate need to know.
- **Take responsibility.** Don't hide behind HR, company policies, or other leaders. As a manager, I have a responsibility to participate in the conversation and handle it respectfully.

Every situation is different. These principles should guide our approach, while the specific process must follow organizational policies and applicable legal requirements.

## Before the Conversation

Preparation matters.

Once a decision has been finalized, I want to make sure we've thoughtfully considered how to communicate it and what support the person will need.

### Coordinate With People Ops

People Ops should be involved in preparing for an involuntary separation.

Before scheduling the conversation, work together to establish:

- The reason for the separation and what can appropriately be communicated.
- The timing and format of the conversation.
- Who should participate and what responsibilities each person has.
- What documentation or approvals are required.
- What information needs to be provided about compensation, benefits, and next steps.
- How access, equipment, and other operational matters will be handled.
- Any circumstances that require additional consideration or support.

I don't believe managers should improvise these decisions or independently establish procedures that belong to People Ops.

### Prepare What You're Going to Say

Know what you need to communicate before entering the conversation.

The message should be clear, respectful, and concise.

Avoid lengthy explanations that make the decision harder to understand or appear open to negotiation when it isn't.

Be prepared to answer questions honestly, while recognizing that some questions may need to be handled by People Ops.

### Consider the Person's Experience

Think about how the conversation will affect the person receiving the news.

- Are we communicating through an appropriate medium?
- Have we chosen a time that avoids unnecessary hardship?
- Will the person have an opportunity to ask questions?
- Do we have the information they need about what happens next?
- Are we treating them with the same respect we'd want for ourselves?

For remote employees, a private video conversation is often appropriate, but individual circumstances and organizational requirements should guide the decision.

## Having the Conversation

### Be Direct

Don't begin with unnecessary small talk or attempt to make the conversation seem like a routine meeting.

Communicate the decision early and clearly.

The person shouldn't have to guess why the meeting was scheduled or whether their employment is ending.

Be compassionate, but don't allow discomfort to make the message ambiguous.

### Explain What You Can

Provide a clear explanation consistent with the circumstances and the information you're authorized to share.

Avoid personal judgments, speculation, blame, or unnecessary criticism.

There may be differences between a performance-related separation, a position elimination, and other circumstances. Each requires an appropriate explanation.

The person deserves honesty, even when the message is difficult.

### Give Them Space

After communicating the decision, allow the person time to process what they've heard.

They may be upset, angry, surprised, emotional, or quiet.

Don't rush to fill every silence or attempt to control their reaction.

Listen to their questions and concerns without becoming defensive.

We may not be able to change the outcome, but we can still treat their response with empathy and respect.

### Explain the Next Steps

Make sure the person understands what happens next.

People Ops should help provide information about:

- Final compensation and any applicable severance.
- Benefits and relevant deadlines.
- Equipment and company property.
- Access to systems and information.
- Relevant documents and contacts for follow-up questions.

Don't make commitments about compensation, references, benefits, or other support unless those commitments have been authorized and can be honored.

If you don't have an answer, acknowledge that and explain who can help.

### End With Respect

Allow the person an appropriate opportunity to ask questions and understand the next steps.

Don't force an optimistic conclusion or expect them to feel grateful or reassured.

We should recognize their contributions where appropriate, but avoid making the conversation about our own feelings.

The priority is ensuring they have been treated respectfully and have the information they need.

## After the Conversation

Our responsibility doesn't end when the meeting does.

### Coordinate the Transition

Work with People Ops and the appropriate teams to complete the offboarding process.

This may include:

- Handling system access according to established security procedures.
- Coordinating equipment returns.
- Addressing outstanding administrative matters.
- Identifying necessary work transitions and ownership changes.
- Ensuring the person has access to the appropriate follow-up information.

Access and information handling should follow company security and privacy policies. We shouldn't automatically forward someone's email or expose their private communications.

### Communicate With the Team

The remaining team deserves appropriate communication about changes that affect them.

However, the departing employee's privacy matters.

Share only what is necessary to explain the transition, changes in responsibilities, and how work will continue.

Avoid speculation or discussing confidential details about the separation.

Be available for questions and recognize that departures can affect team morale, trust, and people's sense of security.

### Support the Remaining Team

Losing a teammate can be difficult for the people who remain.

They may have questions about the decision, concerns about their own future, or anxiety about additional responsibilities.

We won't always be able to share every detail, but we should be as transparent as we can while respecting confidentiality.

Listen to concerns, clarify expectations, and provide support as people adjust.

## Learn From the Experience

After the immediate situation has been handled, I think it's important to reflect on what we've learned.

Depending on the circumstances, some useful questions include:

- Were expectations and feedback communicated clearly?
- Did we provide appropriate support and opportunities to improve?
- Were there organizational or systemic factors that contributed to the situation?
- Did we handle the separation with compassion and respect?
- What could we do differently to improve the experience or prevent similar problems?

This reflection isn't about second-guessing every decision or assigning blame.

It's about becoming better managers and improving the systems and practices that support our teams.

## What Success Looks Like

There's no such thing as a good outcome for everyone in every employment separation.

Someone may be hurt, disappointed, angry, or uncertain about their future. We shouldn't expect otherwise.

What we can control is how we approach the situation.

I want people to be treated with honesty, compassion, dignity, and respect, regardless of the circumstances surrounding their departure.

I also want the remaining team to feel supported and confident that difficult decisions will be handled thoughtfully.

**How we treat people when they're leaving says just as much about our values as how we treat them while they're here.**
